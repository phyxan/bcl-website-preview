Falsely Accused of Child Pornography in Texas

Peter M. Barrett, Attorney Licensed in Texas since 1994 · 150+ jury trials · Dallas, Texas
✓ Statutes checked Texas codes verified September 2026
Straight Answer

Can you be charged with child pornography for files you did not know were there?

You can be investigated and even charged, because files on a device point toward its owner. But Texas and federal law both require proof that the person acted knowingly. If files came through a shared device, Wi-Fi, file sharing, cloud sync, or malware, who put them there is the key question.

TL;DR
  • Texas and federal law both punish possession, and both require knowing conduct.
  • Federal receipt or distribution carries 5 to 20 years. Possession carries up to 10, or up to 20 in some cases.
  • Shared devices, open Wi-Fi, file sharing, cloud sync, and malware can put files where an owner never looked.
  • If police arrive, do not answer questions, do not consent beyond the warrant, and do not delete anything.
  • An independent forensic review examines which account, program, and time put a file on a device.

Being falsely accused of child pornography is frightening, especially when you do not know how the files got there. This page covers how it happens, what the law requires, and what to do. The law turns on knowing possession

Texas law

Texas Penal Code §43.26(a-1) applies when a person "intentionally or knowingly possesses, or intentionally or knowingly accesses with intent to view," visual material of a child engaging in sexual conduct. The person must also know, or should have known, that the child was under 18 when the image was made. Visual material includes a digital file that can be shown on a screen (§43.26(a)(5)(B)).

Texas treats this offense as a felony. The Legislature rewrote §43.26 effective September 1, 2025. It added offenses for AI and other software-made images of a "computer-generated child" (§43.26(a)(2) and (a-2)). Two 2025 bills changed the punishment rules in different ways, so this page does not list Texas punishment levels. A separate section, §43.235, covers obscene material that appears to show a child, including cartoons and AI images (§43.235(b)(1)).

Federal law

18 U.S.C. §2252(a)(2) punishes anyone who "knowingly receives, or distributes" a visual depiction of a minor engaging in sexually explicit conduct, including by computer. Section 2252(a)(4)(B) covers anyone who "knowingly possesses, or knowingly accesses with intent to view" such material. Section 2252A(a)(2) and (a)(5)(B) use the same "knowingly" wording. The Justice Department says federal jurisdiction almost always applies when the internet is used. See our federal criminal defense page.

So a file on a device is not enough by itself. The prosecution must prove the person acted knowingly.

Ranges come from §2252(b) and §2252A(b). Fines can also apply. Qualifying priors include the state and federal sex offense and child pornography convictions the statutes list. Federal felony charges under chapter 110, which includes both sections, have no time limit (18 U.S.C. §3299).

Texas time limits and registration

  • Seven years. A Texas indictment under §43.26 must generally come within seven years of the offense (Tex. Code Crim. Proc. art. 12.01(3)(F)).
  • Deferred adjudication counts. A conviction or deferred adjudication under §43.26 triggers sex offender registration (art. 62.001(5)(B)).
  • Generally for life. The duty to register for §43.26 generally ends only at death (art. 62.101(a)(2)).

A federal conviction with substantially similar elements is also reportable in Texas, unless it resulted in deferred adjudication (art. 62.001(5)(H)). See our guide to Texas sex offender registration.

How innocent people get caught up

Evidence can tie a file to a device, an account, or an internet connection. None of those is a person.

Shared computers and family devices

Many homes share a laptop, tablet, or desktop. Roommates, relatives, and guests may use the same machine. The question becomes which person downloaded, opened, or saved a file, and when.

Shared or unsecured Wi-Fi

Federal cybersecurity guidance warns that anyone in range of an unsecured network can use it, including for illegal activity. It puts typical indoor range at 150 to 300 feet. The FTC adds that a crime committed over your network could be traced back to you. The name on the internet account may point to the wrong person.

Peer-to-peer file sharing

The FTC explains that peer-to-peer programs download files and also make files available to others. People can share folders by mistake, and malware can change which folders are shared. Federal law treats receiving and distributing as separate crimes. So what a program was set to do, and whether the user knew, can matter.

Cloud accounts and automatic sync

Apple explains that iCloud Photos keeps every photo and video in iCloud. Changes on one device show up on other devices signed in to the same account. A family sharing one account can end up with the same files on several devices.

Messaging apps that save received media

Some messaging apps save photos and videos people send you into your phone's gallery. Tech guides report that WhatsApp usually does this by default. A file can land on a phone because someone else sent it.

Malware and remote access

In 2008, Computerworld reported that Massachusetts prosecutors dropped a possession charge against state employee Michael Fiola. His work laptop was badly configured and full of malicious software. A first state examination pointed to Fiola. A second reached the opposite conclusion, and the state said it could not prove he downloaded the images.

Used or secondhand devices

The FTC warns that a computer can hold personal files and photos. It tells owners to erase the hard drive before getting rid of it. Not everyone does. A used device can arrive holding a previous owner's files.

What investigators typically do

Officers often arrive with a search warrant. The Fourth Amendment requires a warrant to particularly describe the place to be searched and the things to be seized. A Justice Department manual explains that computers can be searched effectively off-site with forensic tools. So agents are particularly likely to seize them. Examiners can make a forensic image: an exact copy of the whole drive, including deleted file fragments.

What to do if police arrive or ask for an interview

  • Do not interfere. Stay calm, ask to see the warrant, and let officers do what it allows.
  • Do not agree to more. If asked to search places or devices the warrant does not describe, politely decline and ask for a lawyer.
  • Do not answer questions. The Fifth Amendment protects you from being compelled to be a witness against yourself. Read about your right to remain silent in Texas.
  • Do not delete, wipe, or move anything. Texas Penal Code §37.09 makes destroying or concealing evidence a third-degree felony. It applies once you know an investigation is pending.
  • Write down who uses each device and your Wi-Fi. Share that list only with your lawyer.

If you accidentally downloaded child pornography

Federal law has a narrow affirmative defense to possession (§2252(c); §2252A(d)). The person must have had fewer than three images or items. They must also have acted promptly and in good faith, without keeping or sharing them. And they must have either destroyed each one or reported it to law enforcement and given the agency access.

Section 43.26 has no matching defense. Its listed defenses cover officials doing their jobs, and a close-in-age defense for computer-generated images only (§43.26(h), (h-1), and (h-2)). Section 37.09 also makes destroying evidence a crime. These rules pull in different directions, so talk to a lawyer before you delete, move, or report anything.

How the defense uses independent digital forensics

The State's forensic report is not the last word. In the Fiola case, the second examination disagreed with the first. We have the drive image reviewed by an independent forensic examiner, who looks at:

  • File paths: whether a file sits in a download, sync, or messaging folder.
  • Dates and user accounts: when files arrived, under which login, and where the accused was then.
  • Whether files were opened: recent-file lists, viewing history, and thumbnail caches.
  • File-sharing settings: which folders a program shared and what it was set to download.
  • Malware and remote access: whether someone else could have controlled the device.

Common questions

Can you go to jail for accidentally downloading child pornography?

Texas and federal law punish knowing possession and receipt, so the key question is what you knew and when. Federal law has a narrow defense for fewer than three images promptly destroyed or reported. Section 43.26 has no matching defense. Talk to a lawyer before you delete or report anything.

What does 18 U.S.C. §2252 cover?

It punishes knowingly transporting, receiving, distributing, selling, or possessing depictions of minors in sexually explicit conduct. Receipt or distribution carries 5 to 20 years. Possession carries up to 10 years, or up to 20 if an image involved a child under 12. Prior convictions raise the ranges.

Can someone be charged because of what happened on their Wi-Fi?

An investigation can start with the name on the internet account, but a connection is not a person. Anyone in range of an unsecured network can use it. The prosecution still must prove the accused acted knowingly.

Is child pornography a felony in Texas?

Yes. Possession or promotion under Penal Code §43.26 is a felony. A conviction or deferred adjudication generally means lifetime sex offender registration. An indictment must generally come within seven years.

Should I talk to police if they seize my computer?

No, not without a lawyer. You cannot be compelled to be a witness against yourself. Be polite, do not agree to anything beyond the warrant, do not delete anything, and call a lawyer.
