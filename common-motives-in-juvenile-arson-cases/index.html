Juvenile Arson in Texas: Motives and Consequences

Peter M. Barrett, Attorney Licensed in Texas since 1994 · 150+ jury trials · Dallas, Texas
✓ Statutes checked Texas codes verified September 2026
Straight Answer

Why do juveniles set fires, and what do they face in Texas?

Motive tracks age: curiosity in young children, frustration and conflict from around eight to twelve, and deliberate fire setting in the teens. The law does not grade by motive. Arson under Tex. Penal Code §28.02 is a second degree felony, first degree if anyone is hurt or the target was a home or place of worship, and a person aged 10 to 16 is handled in juvenile court, where the focus is rehabilitation but the consequences are real, including certification as an adult for the most serious cases.

TL;DR
  • Juveniles account for nearly half of arson arrests, the highest juvenile share of any index crime.
  • A large slice of juvenile fire incidents is accidental or undetermined, which matters, because arson requires intent.
  • Texas juvenile court covers conduct from age 10 through 16. At 17, arson is charged in adult court.
  • Arson of a habitation is a first degree felony. So is any arson where someone is injured.
  • From age 14, the State can ask the juvenile court to certify a child for adult prosecution of a first degree felony.

A child accused of setting a fire sits at the intersection of two systems: fire investigation, which decides what the fire was, and juvenile justice, which decides what happens next. Two of those bars carry the defense inside them. Almost one in three juvenile involved fire incidents is ruled accidental, and another 15 percent are undetermined. Arson requires an intentional act, starting a fire with intent to destroy or damage. A bedroom experiment that got away from a ten year old is a tragedy and possibly a negligence claim. It is not automatically a crime.

Motive follows age. Under about seven, fire setting is overwhelmingly curiosity. From roughly eight to twelve, it begins to track psychosocial conflict: a divorce, a move, bullying, frustration without an outlet. In the teens it can become deliberate, by young people who understand the danger, which is also when the justice system's patience runs out. Where a child falls on that arc shapes everything: whether intent can be proven, and what a court should do about it.

How Texas grades arson

Section 28.02 is built on intent: starting a fire or causing an explosion with intent to destroy or damage a structure, vegetation on open space land, or a building, habitation or vehicle in the listed circumstances.

Notice what the first degree bracket covers: the family home is a habitation. A teenager who sets a fire inside the house they live in can face the same grade as a professional torching a building for insurance. What each degree carries is set out in Texas misdemeanors and felonies.

What actually happens to a juvenile

Under Fam. Code §51.02, juvenile court covers children aged 10 or older and under 17 at the time of the conduct. At 17, Texas charges adults. A juvenile arson case moves like this:

  1. Referral and detention decision. Police refer the case to the juvenile department, which decides whether the child is released to parents or detained pending a prompt detention hearing.
  2. Petition. The State files a petition alleging delinquent conduct, the juvenile system's counterpart to an indictment.
  3. Adjudication hearing. The trial. The State must prove the conduct beyond a reasonable doubt, and the child has the right to counsel and to a jury on adjudication.
  4. Disposition. Under §54.04, outcomes run from probation at home, with conditions like counseling and a fire intervention program, through placement, to commitment to the Texas Juvenile Justice Department for serious cases.
  5. Afterwards. Juvenile records are confidential but not invisible, and sealing them later is its own process worth doing properly.

Certification as an adult is the exception that changes everything. Under §54.02, the juvenile court can waive its jurisdiction and transfer a child to adult criminal court: from age 14 for a first degree felony, which includes arson of a habitation, and from age 15 for second and third degree and state jail felonies. Fighting certification is often the most important hearing in the whole case, because it decides which system, and which punishment ranges, apply.

Where these cases are defended

  • Cause and origin. Fire investigation is opinion evidence, not observed fact, and its methodology can be challenged. Accidental and undetermined are the correct findings in a large share of juvenile incidents.
  • Intent. The State must prove intent to destroy or damage. Curiosity, panic, or a fire that spread beyond a small act is a different mental state.
  • The statements. Children explain, apologise and agree with adults in authority. Juvenile confessions have their own admissibility rules, and what a scared 12 year old told a fire marshal is worth a hard look. See Miranda rights.
  • The intervention alternative. Juvenile fire setter intervention programs exist across North Texas, and steering a case toward assessment and intervention rather than adjudication is often the outcome most worth pursuing for a young child.

Common questions

Can a juvenile be tried as an adult for arson in Texas?

Yes, by certification under Family Code section 54.02. From age 14 the juvenile court can waive jurisdiction for a first degree felony, which includes arson of a habitation or any arson causing injury, and from 15 for lesser felonies. Certification requires a full hearing, and it can be fought.

What happens to a 12 year old who sets a fire?

A child of 10 to 16 goes through juvenile court, where the range runs from dismissal or a fire intervention program through probation to commitment for serious cases. Under 10, Texas has no criminal jurisdiction at all, and the response is family services, not court.

Is it still arson if the fire was an accident?

No. Arson under section 28.02 requires starting the fire with intent to destroy or damage. Accidental and undetermined causes together account for a large share of juvenile fire incidents, which is why the cause and origin investigation deserves as much scrutiny as the legal case.

Do parents have to pay for the damage?

Texas law makes parents civilly liable for property damage from a child's wilful and malicious conduct, within statutory limits, and courts can order restitution as part of a juvenile disposition. The criminal defense and the civil exposure should be handled together.

Will a juvenile arson case follow my child forever?

Juvenile records are confidential but real: they surface in later juvenile or criminal proceedings and some licensing contexts. Many can be sealed after the case ends and eligibility rules are met. Certification and commitment cases carry the heaviest long term weight, which is one more reason to fight the grade and the forum early.
