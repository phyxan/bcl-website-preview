Third DWI Penalties in Texas

Peter M. Barrett, Attorney Licensed in Texas since 1994 · 150+ jury trials · Dallas, Texas
✓ Statutes checked Texas codes verified September 2026
Straight Answer

What does a third DWI carry in Texas?

A third DWI is a felony of the third degree under Tex. Penal Code §49.09(b)(2): 2 to 10 years in prison and a fine of up to $10,000. On top of that sit a state fine of up to $6,000 under the Transportation Code, a licence suspension of 180 days to 2 years, and an ignition interlock ordered while the case is still on bond. With one prior felony conviction the range climbs to 2 to 20 years.

TL;DR
  • Two prior intoxication convictions make the third charge a felony, no matter how old the priors are.
  • The range is 2 to 10 years, and prior felony convictions can raise it under the habitual offender rules.
  • An ignition interlock is required as a bond condition on any repeat DWI charge, installed within 30 days at your expense.
  • The licence suspension runs 180 days to 2 years, and the ALR clock starts at arrest, not at trial.
  • The priors themselves are attackable. If one falls, the felony falls with it.

A third DWI is the point where the system stops treating the case as a misdemeanor problem and starts treating the person as the problem. What makes the third one a felony

A first DWI under §49.04 is a Class B misdemeanor with a 72 hour minimum term. A second is a Class A misdemeanor with a 30 day minimum under §49.09(a). The felony trigger is §49.09(b)(2): the charge becomes a third degree felony when the person has twice before been convicted of any offense relating to operating a motor vehicle while intoxicated, and the statute sweeps in boating, flying and even amusement ride versions, plus equivalent convictions from other states.

Two things people get wrong about the priors. There is no ten year washout: convictions from the 1990s still count. And a prior counts as a conviction even where probation was completed, with narrow exceptions that depend on exactly how the judgment was written. That last point is not fine print. It is a defense.

The full penalty stack

  • Prison: 2 to 10 years in the Texas Department of Criminal Justice, and a fine of up to $10,000 (§12.34).
  • Enhancement: one prior felony conviction moves the range to that of a second degree felony, 2 to 20 years, under §12.42(a). Two sequential prior felonies make it 25 to life under §12.42(d). A prior felony DWI counts.
  • State fine: on final conviction, Transp. Code §709.001 adds $3,000 for a first conviction in 36 months, $4,500 for a second or later, and $6,000 where the alcohol concentration was 0.15 or more. This is on top of the criminal fine.
  • Licence: suspension of 180 days to 2 years on conviction under Transp. Code §521.344(a)(1)(B). The separate ALR administrative suspension starts from the arrest, and you have 15 days to demand the hearing that contests it.
  • Interlock before trial: on a repeat DWI charge, Code Crim. Proc. art. 17.441 requires the magistrate to order an ignition interlock as a bond condition, installed within 30 days of release, at your expense.
  • Afterwards: a felony conviction touches firearm rights, licences and employment. The knock-on effects are the same ones set out in our guide to felony consequences.

Is probation possible on a third DWI?

Often yes, and it is frequently the real fight. Community supervision on a felony DWI typically carries conditions with teeth: jail time as a condition, an interlock, treatment, and years of supervision. Deferred adjudication is not available for a DWI with two priors. Whether probation is on the table depends on the record, the county and the judge, which is exactly the terrain a local trial lawyer knows. See community supervision explained.

Where third DWI cases are actually won

  • The priors. The State must prove both prior convictions properly. Old judgments with missing signatures, uncounseled pleas, or paperwork that does not match the person are not automatic proof. Knock out one prior and the felony becomes a misdemeanor.
  • The stop. No valid reason for the stop, no case. The video often disagrees with the report.
  • The testing. Field sobriety tests have standardised procedures that officers skip, and blood draws have warrants, timelines and lab work that can each fail. See the complete DWI defense guide.
  • The intoxication element itself. Driving badly is not the offense. The State must prove intoxication at the time of driving, and gaps between driving and testing are real gaps.
The two clocks that start at arrest

The ALR licence clock gives you 15 days to request a hearing or the suspension happens by default. And the interlock order lands at the first bond setting. Common questions

Is a third DWI always a felony in Texas?

Yes. Section 49.09(b)(2) makes the offense a third degree felony once the State proves two prior convictions for operating a vehicle while intoxicated, in any combination of Texas and out-of-state equivalents. There is no time limit on how old the priors can be.

How much jail time does a third DWI carry?

The range is 2 to 10 years. With one prior felony conviction it is punished as a second degree felony, 2 to 20 years, and two sequential prior felonies raise the floor to 25 years under the habitual offender provision.

Can I get probation for a third DWI in Texas?

Regular community supervision is often possible depending on your record and the county, but deferred adjudication is not available. Felony DWI probation usually includes jail days as a condition, an interlock and treatment, and a violation can convert it into the prison range.

How long is the licence suspension for a third DWI?

On conviction, 180 days to 2 years under Transportation Code section 521.344. The administrative ALR suspension is separate, starts from the arrest, and is contested at its own hearing that must be requested within 15 days.

Do old DWI convictions really count against me?

Yes. Texas removed the washout period, so convictions of any age count for enhancement. But old priors are also the most vulnerable to attack, because records go missing and old judgments carry defects. Challenging the priors is often the strongest move in the case.
