Understanding Your Miranda Rights

Peter M. Barrett, Attorney Licensed in Texas since 1994 · 150+ jury trials · Dallas, Texas
Straight Answer

When do police have to read you your Miranda rights?

Before custodial interrogation: when you are in custody and police question you. The warnings are the right to remain silent, that anything you say can be used against you, the right to a lawyer, and a lawyer appointed if you cannot afford one. In Texas, a statement from custodial questioning is admissible only if those warnings and the right to end the interview were given and waived, and an oral statement must be electronically recorded (Code of Criminal Procedure article 38.22).

TL;DR
  • Miranda warnings are required before custodial interrogation, meaning you are in custody and being questioned.
  • The four warnings: the right to remain silent, anything you say can be used against you, the right to a lawyer, and a lawyer appointed if you cannot afford one.
  • Texas adds the right to end the interview at any time, and requires oral statements to be recorded to be used.
  • Skipping the warnings does not dismiss the case, but statements from that questioning are generally kept out of the prosecution's case.
  • Routine booking questions and questions about an immediate public safety threat are exceptions.

Most people expect the arresting officer to read them their Miranda rights. In the event that your rights have not been read, that isn’t to say you can escape your
punishment. If police question you in custody without giving the warnings, what you say in that questioning generally cannot be used to prove the case against you, although it can still be used to challenge your testimony if you testify. In this blog, our Dallas criminal defense attorney explains what your Miranda rights are, when they’re required, and the exceptions.

What are your rights?

The Miranda rights were first established in a U.S. Supreme Court’s decision of Miranda v. Arizona. Under the law, police must warn a person who is in custody before questioning them. Before that questioning, police must let him or her know that they have a right to remain silent, anything they say can be used against them, they have a right to an attorney, and that they will be given an attorney if they cannot afford one.

When are Miranda rights required?

Miranda warnings are required when two things are true: you are in police custody, and police are questioning you. If you are in custody but nobody questions you, or you are questioned when you are not in custody, Miranda
rights are not required. If you find yourself being asked questions by an officer, you are not obligated to answer them if you are not in their custody. Oftentimes police will avoid placing suspects under arrest to
avoid reading them their rights, in the hopes they say something incriminating.

What are the exceptions?

There are several instances in which police are not required to read your Miranda rights. Routine booking questions, such as your name, address, height, weight and date of birth, have been allowed without warnings. And when there is an immediate threat to public safety, officers may ask questions prompted by that danger before giving the warnings, and the answers can be used.

Common questions

Does a Miranda violation get my case dismissed?

No. The usual remedy is that statements from un-warned custodial questioning cannot be used to prove the case against you. They can still be used to challenge your credibility if you testify, and statements you volunteer without being questioned are not covered.

Do police have to read my rights when they arrest me?

Not at the moment of arrest. The warnings are required before police question someone who is in custody. If nobody questions you, no warning is needed.

What does Texas add to Miranda?

Under Code of Criminal Procedure article 38.22, a written statement from custodial questioning must show the warnings, including the right to end the interview, and an oral statement must be electronically recorded with the warnings given and waived on the recording, subject to limited exceptions.

Are there exceptions to Miranda?

Yes. Routine booking questions, such as name, address and date of birth, have been allowed without warnings, and officers may ask questions prompted by an immediate threat to public safety before warning a suspect.
