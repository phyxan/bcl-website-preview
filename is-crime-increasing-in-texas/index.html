Is Crime Increasing in Texas?

Peter M. Barrett, Attorney Licensed in Texas since 1994 · 150+ jury trials · Dallas, Texas
✓ Statutes checked Texas codes verified September 2026
Straight Answer

Is crime increasing in Texas?

No. The state's own numbers say the opposite. The Texas DPS 2024 Crime in Texas report shows the violent crime rate down 5.4 percent from 2023, the property crime rate down 9.4 percent, and murder down 16.9 percent. Of every tracked category, only two rose: commercial sex trafficking and arson. Perception runs the other way, and that gap matters if you are the person charged, because juries and judges arrive carrying the perception.

TL;DR
  • Violent crime rate in 2024: 382.5 per 100,000, down from 404.2. Property crime: 2,094 per 100,000, down from 2,312.
  • Murder fell 16.9 percent, robbery 8.5, motor vehicle theft 19.6, burglary 7.0.
  • The only increases: commercial sex trafficking (up 4.8 percent) and arson (up 2.2 percent).
  • Falling crime does not mean falling prosecution. Enforcement priorities concentrate on fewer defendants.
  • If you are one of them, the trend line is background noise. The charge in front of you is the whole story.

People ask this question because it feels like the answer should be yes. The honest answer comes from the state's own annual report, compiled by the Department of Public Safety from every reporting agency in Texas. Here is what the current one says, and what it means practically for anyone facing a charge in Dallas County. Peter M. Why it does not feel that way

Crime perception tracks coverage and feeds, not rates. A carjacking video travels statewide in an afternoon; a 19.6 percent drop in vehicle theft is a spreadsheet row. There is no conspiracy in that, but there is a consequence: the people who eventually sit on juries, set bonds and write charging policies marinate in the perception, not the data. Defense lawyers deal with the perception every day, which is one reason voir dire and bond arguments lean on numbers like the ones above.

What falling crime means if you are charged

  • Enforcement concentrates. Fewer offenses with steady enforcement budgets means more attention per case: task forces, longer investigations, and stacked charges where one count would once have done. The trafficking row above is where several of those task forces now live.
  • Politics lag the data. Punishment ranges and bail policy are set by the perception cycle. Nothing about a falling murder rate softens the range on the indictment in front of you: see what each charge carries.
  • Your case is a sample of one. Trend lines neither convict nor acquit. The stop, the statements, the lab work and the witnesses decide your case, the same as in any year: start at arrested in Dallas, now what.

Common questions

What is the crime rate in Texas right now?

The most recent full-year figures, from the DPS 2024 report, put violent crime at 382.5 per 100,000 and property crime at 2,094 per 100,000, both down from 2023. New annual reports publish each year on the DPS crime records site linked in Sources.

Is Dallas more dangerous than the rest of Texas?

Big cities carry higher raw volumes, and headline comparisons usually ignore rate versus volume. The honest way to compare is rates per 100,000 from the DPS report's jurisdiction tables rather than totals, and even large agencies showed declines in the current report.

If crime is falling, why are police and prosecutors charging harder?

Because enforcement capacity does not shrink with the crime rate. Task forces and prosecutorial units concentrate on fewer cases, which shows up as longer investigations, more counts per indictment, and heavier use of enhancements. The trend helps the state's statistics and does nothing for the individual defendant.

Which crimes are actually increasing in Texas?

In the 2024 DPS data, two categories rose: human trafficking involving commercial sex acts, up 4.8 percent by rate, and arson, up 2.2 percent. Everything else in the rate table declined, including every major violent and property category.

Where do these numbers come from?

The DPS Crime in Texas annual report, which compiles incident-based reporting from Texas law enforcement agencies. It is the state's official series, published on the DPS crime records site, and the exact edition used here is linked in Sources.
