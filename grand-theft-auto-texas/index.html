Grand Theft Auto in Texas

Peter M. Barrett, Attorney Licensed in Texas since 1994 · 150+ jury trials · Dallas, Texas
✓ Statutes checked Texas codes verified September 2026
Straight Answer

What is grand theft auto called in Texas?

Texas law has no offense named grand theft auto. Taking a vehicle is ordinary theft under Tex. Penal Code §31.03, graded by the vehicle's value: $2,500 to $30,000 is a state jail felony, $30,000 to $150,000 is a third degree felony. Driving someone's vehicle without consent, even with no intent to keep it, is a separate state jail felony called unauthorised use of a motor vehicle under §31.07.

TL;DR
  • "Grand theft auto" is a California term and a video game. Texas charges theft, graded by value.
  • Most car theft cases are state jail felonies, 180 days to 2 years, because most cars fall between $2,500 and $30,000.
  • A $30,000 truck moves the case to a third degree felony, 2 to 10 years.
  • Joyriding is not a lesser ticket. Unauthorised use is its own state jail felony, with no proof of intent to steal required.
  • The fights are consent, intent and value, and value is more negotiable than people think.

People arrested over a vehicle usually search for the phrase they know from movies. What Texas actually charges is either theft or unauthorised use, and the difference between those two counts, and between the value brackets inside theft, is the difference between county jail and prison. Vehicle theft under section 31.03

Theft is appropriating property without the owner's effective consent and with intent to deprive the owner of it. For a vehicle, the grade follows the value ladder in §31.03(e):

  • under $2,500: a misdemeanor, from Class C to Class A by value;
  • $2,500 to less than $30,000: state jail felony, 180 days to 2 years in a state jail and up to a $10,000 fine;
  • $30,000 to less than $150,000: third degree felony, 2 to 10 years;
  • $150,000 to less than $300,000: second degree felony, 2 to 20 years;
  • $300,000 or more: first degree felony.

Notice where ordinary cars land. A ten year old sedan is a state jail felony. A new truck is a third degree felony. The State's value figure usually comes from an owner's estimate or a quick book lookup, and contesting it is not a technicality: at $29,000 against $31,000, the two numbers are different punishment ranges. Prior theft convictions also move the grade, and under §12.35(c) a deadly weapon finding turns a state jail felony into a third degree felony.

Joyriding: unauthorised use of a motor vehicle

§31.07 is the charge people underestimate. A person commits it by intentionally or knowingly operating another's boat, airplane or motor-propelled vehicle without the owner's effective consent. That is the whole offense. The State does not have to prove you meant to keep or sell the vehicle, only that you drove it knowing you did not have consent. It is a state jail felony, same range as stealing a $25,000 car.

UUMV is where borrowed-car situations become criminal cases: the ex who kept driving the car after the breakup, the friend who did not return it, the buyer whose payment fell through. It is also the charge that turns a passenger's bad night into a felony, because "operating" and "knowing" get argued on thin facts.

Where these cases are won

  • Consent. Most vehicle cases are relationship cases. Consent given once and never clearly withdrawn, shared use, a co-signed loan, or a title dispute is not theft, it is a civil argument the State is being asked to referee with a felony.
  • Intent to deprive. Keeping a car late is not the same as intending the owner never gets it back. For UUMV, the State must prove you knew consent was absent, and belief that you had permission defeats it.
  • Value. The number on the indictment is an allegation, not a fact. Book value, condition and mileage move cases across bracket lines.
  • Possession is not proof of theft. Being found in a stolen vehicle days later raises a fact question about what you knew, and knowing receipt is a different fight from taking.
  • The stop and the statements. Vehicle cases start with traffic stops and end with roadside questions. What was said before Miranda warnings matters, and so does whether the warnings came at all.

Common questions

Is stealing a car a felony in Texas?

Almost always. Theft is graded by value under Penal Code section 31.03, and any vehicle worth $2,500 or more makes the case at least a state jail felony. Only a vehicle worth less than $2,500, with no prior theft convictions, stays a misdemeanor.

What is the punishment for car theft in Texas?

A state jail felony, 180 days to 2 years, for a vehicle worth $2,500 to $30,000. From $30,000 the range is 2 to 10 years, and it keeps climbing with value. A fine of up to $10,000 can be added at every felony level.

Is joyriding a crime in Texas even if I meant to return the car?

Yes. Unauthorised use of a motor vehicle under section 31.07 does not require intent to steal, only operating the vehicle knowing the owner had not consented. It is a state jail felony on its own.

Can I be charged if I did not know the car was stolen?

Knowledge is the State's burden. Being a passenger, or driving a car you borrowed from someone else, is not proof you knew its history. These cases turn on what you were told, what you paid, and what the circumstances would signal to a reasonable person.

What if the car is partly mine, like a car I co-signed for?

Ownership and consent get murky with co-signers, spouses and financed vehicles, and murky is the defense's friend. Theft requires taking property of another without effective consent, and a genuine ownership dispute belongs in civil court, not a felony courtroom.
