How Long Is a Prison Sentence for Tax Evasion?

Peter M. Barrett, Attorney Licensed in Texas since 1994 · 150+ jury trials · Dallas, Texas
✓ Statutes checked Texas codes verified September 2026
Straight Answer

How long is a prison sentence for tax evasion?

Federal tax evasion is a felony with a maximum of 5 years in prison, a fine, or both. There is no mandatory minimum. In fiscal year 2025, 68% of people sentenced for federal tax fraud offenses went to prison, and the average sentence was 17 months, according to the U.S. Sentencing Commission.

TL;DR
  • Tax evasion under 26 U.S.C. §7201 is a federal felony: up to 5 years in prison and a fine of up to $100,000 ($500,000 for a corporation), plus the costs of prosecution.
  • In fiscal year 2025, 68% of people sentenced for tax fraud went to prison. The average sentence was 17 months.
  • The line between a civil tax problem and a crime is willfulness. The IRS points people with honest mistakes to options such as amended returns.
  • The government generally has 6 years to charge tax evasion.

Tax evasion is a federal felony

The main tax evasion law is 26 U.S.C. §7201. It applies to any person who "willfully attempts in any manner to evade or defeat any tax" or its payment. A person convicted under this law is "guilty of a felony."

The penalties written into §7201 are:

  • Prison: not more than 5 years.
  • Fine: not more than $100,000, or $500,000 for a corporation.
  • Both: the court may impose prison and a fine, "together with the costs of prosecution."

The statute uses the words "not more than." It sets a maximum, not a minimum. The Sentencing Commission's fiscal year 2025 data backs this up: no one sentenced for tax fraud that year was convicted of an offense carrying a mandatory minimum penalty.

Related federal tax crimes and their maximums

Tax evasion is not the only federal tax crime. Each related law sets its own maximum.

A few points about these laws:

  • False returns. §7206(1) covers a person who willfully signs a return under penalty of perjury that "he does not believe to be true and correct as to every material matter."
  • Payroll and trust fund taxes. §7202 covers a person required to collect and pay over a tax who "willfully fails" to do so.

Fines can be higher than the tax code says

A general federal law, 18 U.S.C. §3571(b), lets a court fine an individual up to the greatest of these amounts:

  • the amount in the law that defines the offense;
  • for a felony, $250,000;
  • for a Class A misdemeanor, $100,000;
  • under §3571(d), twice the gross gain or twice the gross loss from the offense.

For organizations, §3571(c) sets $500,000 for a felony and $200,000 for a Class A misdemeanor. Under 18 U.S.C. §3559(a), an offense with a maximum of more than six months up to one year is a Class A misdemeanor. That describes a §7203 failure-to-file charge.

What sentences do courts actually give? The federal data

The U.S. Sentencing Commission publishes what courts actually did. Its Quick Facts report on tax fraud offenses for fiscal year 2025 found:

  • 324 of the 66,662 federal cases reported to the Commission involved tax fraud.
  • 68% of people sentenced for tax fraud were sentenced to prison.
  • The average sentence length was 17 months, up from 14 months in fiscal year 2021.
  • The average guideline minimum was 26 months.
  • 43% of sentences were under the guideline range, and 25% were within it.
  • The median loss was $546,562. 28% of cases involved losses over $1.5 million.

Two cautions apply. First, "tax fraud" in this report is broader than §7201. It covers cases sentenced under the guidelines for tax evasion, failure to file, and false returns. Second, the report gives an average, not a median prison term. An average cannot predict any one case.

How a federal judge sets a tax sentence

Federal sentencing uses the U.S. Sentencing Guidelines, and the Commission reports each sentence against its guideline range. The main tax guideline is USSG §2T1.1. It sets the base offense level from the Tax Table in §2T4.1 based on the tax loss. If there is no tax loss, the base level is 6.

The Commission's own background note says this guideline "relies most heavily on the amount of loss." The Tax Table rises with the loss. For example, a tax loss of $2,500 or less is level 6, more than $100,000 is level 16, and more than $1,500,000 is level 22.

Other factors can raise the level. Under §2T1.1(b), the level goes up by 2 if the offense involved "sophisticated means." It also goes up by 2 if the person failed to report more than $10,000 in a year from criminal activity. In fiscal year 2025, the Commission reports that sentences were also increased for things like a leadership or supervisory role (6%) and obstructing justice (4%). It also reports that 56% of tax fraud sentences were downward variances, meaning the judge went below the guideline range.

Civil tax problems versus tax crimes

The tax code also has civil penalties. For example, under 26 U.S.C. §6663, if part of an underpayment is due to fraud, the IRS adds a penalty "equal to 75 percent of the portion of the underpayment which is attributable to fraud." That is a civil penalty, not a prison sentence.

What turns a tax problem into a crime is usually willfulness. The evasion, false return, failure to file, and failure to pay over statutes all use the word "willfully." The IRS describes willfulness as "not simply making a mistake." It calls it "the intentional, purposeful, deliberate act to hide income or assets." The IRS says taxpayers who made a non-willful error should consider options such as amended or delinquent returns.

Statute of limitations for tax crimes

Under 26 U.S.C. §6531, most federal tax crimes must be charged within 3 years. The limit is 6 years for several offenses, including:

  • willfully attempting to evade or defeat any tax or its payment (§7201);
  • willfully failing to pay any tax or make any return;
  • false statements under §7206(1);
  • interfering with IRS officers or employees under §7212(a);
  • offenses involving defrauding or attempting to defraud the United States.

Time a person spends outside the United States, or as a fugitive, does not count toward that period.

What a criminal tax investigation looks like

IRS Criminal Investigation conducts criminal investigations of alleged violations of the tax code. The IRS says a case can start when an auditor or revenue officer "detects possible fraud." Tips from the public and other law enforcement cases are also sources.

The IRS says agents use tools such as witness interviews, search warrants, and bank record subpoenas. If the evidence supports charges, the agent writes a report recommending prosecution. For tax cases it goes to the Department of Justice Tax Division. Each level of review may decide the case should not be prosecuted.

What to do if IRS special agents contact you

If a special agent contacts you, these steps help protect you:

  1. Do not answer questions without a lawyer. You can politely decline to talk and ask for the agent's card. Read about your right to remain silent.
  2. Do not destroy, change, or hide records. Keep your files, emails, and bank records as they are.
  3. Do not file amended returns on your own first. Get advice from a criminal defense lawyer before you contact the IRS or file anything new.

Voluntary disclosure before an investigation starts

IRS Criminal Investigation runs a Voluntary Disclosure Practice for taxpayers whose noncompliance was willful. The IRS says a disclosure is timely only if it arrives before the IRS has started a civil exam or criminal investigation, or has received information about the person's noncompliance from a third party or a criminal enforcement action.

The IRS also says a voluntary disclosure "will not automatically" give immunity from prosecution, though it "may result in prosecution not being recommended." Participants must pay in full, or set up a full-pay installment agreement, for the tax, interest, and penalties. They must also give a statement admitting their willful failure to comply. The practice does not apply to income from illegal sources. The IRS proposed changes to this program in December 2025. Because making a disclosure means admitting willfulness, talk with a lawyer before you apply.

Do you get a criminal record for tax evasion?

Yes, if you are convicted. Section 7201 says a person convicted of tax evasion is "guilty of a felony." That is a federal felony conviction. A §7203 conviction is a federal misdemeanor. See our guide to white collar crimes for related charges.

Common questions

What is the average jail time for tax evasion?

The U.S. Sentencing Commission reports that in fiscal year 2025, the average sentence for federal tax fraud offenses was 17 months, and 68% of people were sentenced to prison. That group includes tax evasion, failure to file, and false return cases.

Can you go to prison for tax evasion?

Yes. Under 26 U.S.C. §7201, tax evasion is a felony punishable by up to 5 years in prison, a fine of up to $100,000 ($500,000 for a corporation), or both, plus the costs of prosecution.

Is there a minimum sentence for tax evasion?

No mandatory minimum is written into §7201. The statute says "not more than 5 years." In fiscal year 2025, the Sentencing Commission reported no tax fraud defendants convicted of an offense carrying a mandatory minimum.

Can you go to jail for not paying taxes?

The crime requires willfulness. A willful failure to file or pay is a misdemeanor under §7203, with up to 1 year in prison. The IRS says people who made non-willful errors should consider options such as amended or delinquent returns.

How far back can the IRS go for criminal tax evasion?

Under 26 U.S.C. §6531, prosecutors generally have 6 years to charge tax evasion. Time spent outside the United States or as a fugitive does not count toward that period.
