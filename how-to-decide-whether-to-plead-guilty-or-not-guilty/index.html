How to Decide Whether to Plead Guilty or Not Guilty

Peter M. Barrett, Attorney Licensed in Texas since 1994 · 150+ jury trials · Dallas, Texas
✓ Statutes checked Texas codes verified September 2026
Straight Answer

Should I plead guilty or not guilty?

At the start of a case, not guilty, almost always. A not guilty plea is not a claim of innocence. It is the move that keeps every option open: discovery, suppression motions, negotiation and trial. A guilty plea can never be taken back easily and can never be improved. Texas law itself treats the decision this seriously: under Tex. Code Crim. Proc. art. 26.13, a judge cannot accept a guilty plea without warning you of the punishment range and the immigration consequences, and without finding the plea free and voluntary.

TL;DR
  • Pleading not guilty first costs you nothing and preserves everything. You can change it later.
  • A guilty plea waives your trial, your jury and most of your appeal, permanently.
  • The State's first offer is a starting number, made before anyone has tested the evidence.
  • Deferred adjudication is a guilty plea with a conditional exit, and it is not available for every offense.
  • The right answer comes from the evidence, the punishment range and the collateral consequences, not from fear at the first setting.

The plea is the single most consequential decision in a criminal case, and it is routinely made worst by people deciding fastest. This page explains what each plea does mechanically, what the law requires before a plea is accepted, and the questions that should be answered before anyone pleads to anything. Peter M. The three pleas, and what each one does

Under art. 27.02, a defendant's pleadings include guilty, not guilty, and nolo contendere.

  • Not guilty puts the State to its burden. It does not lock you into a trial and does not offend the court. It simply means: prove it. Every negotiation that follows happens with your rights intact.
  • Guilty ends the contest. The court moves straight to punishment. You waive the jury, the cross-examination of witnesses, and with narrow exceptions the appeal.
  • Nolo contendere has the same criminal effect as a guilty plea. Its difference is civil: the plea itself cannot be used as an admission in a related civil lawsuit, which matters in cases with a crash or an injury behind them.

What must happen before a guilty plea counts

Article 26.13 requires the judge, before accepting a guilty or nolo plea, to admonish you of the punishment range, and of the fact that if you are not a citizen, the plea may result in deportation, exclusion from admission, or denial of naturalization. The court cannot accept the plea unless you are mentally competent and the plea is free and voluntary. Those warnings exist because courts know what a plea gives up. Treat the solemnity as information.

Reading a plea offer like a lawyer

  • What does the evidence actually look like? An offer made before the video is watched and the lab file is opened is an offer priced on nothing. Suppression issues change offers: see motions to suppress.
  • What is the trial exposure, really? Compare the offer against the realistic range after trial, set out in the punishment ranges, not against the maximum the prosecutor mentions.
  • What kind of supervision is being offered? Straight probation and deferred adjudication are different animals. Deferred can end without a conviction, but it starts with a guilty plea, a violation exposes you to the full range, and eligibility is limited by art. 42A.102, which excludes some offenses, including repeat DWI.
  • What happens after the sentence? Licences, immigration, firearms, employment, and whether the record can ever be sealed. A conviction blocks expunction, and some pleas block nondisclosure. Read clearing your record before pleading, not after.
  • Is the deal in writing, complete, with nothing left "to be discussed"? Terms that are not on the record do not exist.

When each choice tends to be right

Not guilty is right at arraignment in nearly every case, because it is reversible and preserves leverage. It remains right where the evidence has real problems, where the offer does not beat the realistic trial outcome, or where the collateral consequences of the plea are as heavy as the sentence.

A negotiated plea can be right where the evidence is strong and tested, the offer meaningfully beats the trial exposure, and the deal has been checked against every collateral consequence, including the ones that surface years later. The key word is tested. A plea taken at the first setting, before discovery, is not a decision. It is a guess with permanent consequences.

Common questions

Does pleading not guilty make the judge or prosecutor go harder on me?

No. Not guilty at arraignment is the expected, routine plea, entered thousands of times a day. Nobody prices a case worse because you asked the State to prove it. Deals do not disappear because you declined to plead at the first setting.

Can I change my plea later?

Not guilty to guilty: yes, at almost any point, and that is how negotiated cases resolve. Guilty to not guilty: only rarely, before sentencing, at the court's discretion, and almost never after. That asymmetry is the whole argument for starting at not guilty.

What is the difference between deferred adjudication and probation?

Straight probation follows a conviction. Deferred adjudication postpones the finding of guilt while you complete supervision, and can end without a conviction, but it begins with a guilty plea and a violation exposes you to the entire punishment range. Eligibility is limited by article 42A.102.

Should I take a plea deal to just get it over with?

That instinct is how people buy convictions that follow them for decades. The record you accept in twenty minutes affects licences, immigration, housing and employment permanently, and a conviction cannot be expunged. Get the evidence reviewed first. The deal will usually still be there, and it sometimes gets better.

What does an open plea mean?

Pleading guilty without an agreement and letting the judge set punishment after a hearing. It is occasionally the right strategy, but it trades the certainty of a negotiated number for the judge's discretion, so it belongs at the end of careful preparation, not the beginning.
