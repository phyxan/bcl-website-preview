How Do Prosecutors Prove That Someone Is Embezzling?

Peter M. Barrett, Attorney Licensed in Texas since 1994 · 150+ jury trials · Dallas, Texas
✓ Statutes checked Texas codes verified September 2026
Straight Answer

What do prosecutors have to prove in an embezzlement case?

Texas has no crime named embezzlement. It is prosecuted as theft under Penal Code §31.03. The State must prove three things beyond a reasonable doubt. That you appropriated property. That you did it without the owner's effective consent. And that you intended to deprive the owner of it. The dollar value sets the grade. Some cases are charged instead under §32.45, misapplication of fiduciary property.

TL;DR
  • Embezzlement is one of the old offenses folded into the single theft offense by §31.02.
  • The elements are in §31.03(a) and (b)(1): appropriation, no effective consent, intent to deprive.
  • The definitions in §31.01 decide most of these cases, not the word "embezzlement."
  • Value is fair market value at the time and place of the offense (§31.08(a)). Amounts from one scheme can be added together (§31.09).
  • The proof is usually records: ledgers, bank statements, expense reports, access logs, emails, and testimony from co-workers or auditors.

These investigations usually start quietly. An auditor finds a gap, and someone asks about a transaction from two years ago. This page explains what the State has to prove. Peter M. The elements the State must prove

There is no Texas statute called embezzlement. §31.02 says theft, as defined in §31.03, is a single offense. It supersedes several older offenses, and embezzlement is on that list.

Under §31.03(a), a person commits theft if he unlawfully appropriates property with intent to deprive the owner of it. Appropriation is unlawful if it is without the owner's effective consent (§31.03(b)(1)).

"Owner" is defined outside Chapter 31. Under §1.07(a)(35), an owner has title, or possession, whether lawful or not. A greater right to possession than the actor also counts.

The definitions that carry the weight

Most of the argument happens inside four definitions in §31.01.

Appropriate (§31.01(4)). This covers more than pocketing cash. Acquiring or otherwise exercising control over property is enough.

Deprive (§31.01(2)). The statute lists three forms. Withholding property permanently, or so long that a major portion of its value or enjoyment is lost. Restoring it only on payment of a reward or other compensation. Or disposing of it so recovery by the owner is unlikely.

Effective consent (§31.01(3)). Consent includes consent by a person legally authorized to act for the owner. The statute then lists when consent is not effective. Two matter most here. Consent induced by deception or coercion is not effective. Neither is consent from a person the actor knows is not legally authorized to act for the owner.

Deception (§31.01(1)). This has five parts. They include creating or confirming a false impression of law or fact. They also include failing to correct a false impression you created, and preventing someone from getting information likely to affect their judgment. The fifth part has a limit written into it. Failing to carry out a pledged performance, with no other evidence of intent or knowledge, is not sufficient proof.

Coercion comes from §1.07(a)(9). It means a threat, however communicated. The list includes a threat to accuse someone of an offense, or to harm their credit or business repute.

The other charge: misapplication of fiduciary property

Some cases are filed under §32.45. A fiduciary under §32.45(a)(1) includes a trustee, guardian, executor, or receiver. It also covers an employee or agent carrying on fiduciary functions for a fiduciary.

Under §32.45(b), the offense is intentionally, knowingly, or recklessly misapplying property held as a fiduciary, or property of a financial institution. The handling must involve substantial risk of loss to the owner, or to a person the property is held for. "Misapply" means dealing with property contrary to the agreement under which it is held, or contrary to a law governing its custody or disposition (§32.45(a)(2)).

Two differences matter. This statute reaches reckless conduct. And it does not require an actual loss, only a substantial risk of one. The governing agreement becomes the center of the case.

How the amount is measured

Value is not whatever number appears in a company spreadsheet. §31.08(a) sets it as fair market value at the time and place of the offense. If fair market value cannot be ascertained, value is the cost of replacing the property within a reasonable time.

Documents have their own rule (§31.08(b)). If value cannot reasonably be ascertained under any of these rules, §31.08(c) deems the property to have a value of $750 or more but less than $2,500.

Subsection (d) is the one people miss. The accused may prove, by a preponderance of the evidence, that he gave consideration for the property or held a legal interest in it. That amount is then deducted from the value.

Why the total matters more than any single entry

Workplace cases are rarely about one transaction. They are about a pattern of small ones. §31.09 lets the State treat that pattern as one case. Amounts obtained pursuant to one scheme or continuing course of conduct, from the same or several sources, may be aggregated in determining the grade.

That is how a stack of small entries becomes a felony. Under §31.03(e)(4)(A), theft of $2,500 or more but less than $30,000 is a state jail felony. For the full value ladder, see Texas embezzlement charges.

Aggregation is not automatic. The statute requires one scheme or a continuing course of conduct. Whether scattered transactions over years fit that description is worth pressing.

The evidence these cases usually rest on

There is rarely an eyewitness. These cases are built from documents, and a witness then explains them. The material usually includes:

  • Accounting and bank records. Ledger and journal entries, vendor files, payroll registers, canceled checks, card statements, and transfers between accounts.
  • Expense and reimbursement records. Submitted receipts, approval forms, and the policy that was supposed to govern them.
  • Access and system logs. Who logged into the accounting system, when, and what was changed or deleted.
  • Emails, messages, and approvals. Often the only place where authority to spend was given or refused.
  • Testimony from co-workers, supervisors, and auditors. Usually to explain what the records mean and what the rules were.

Two proof rules inside the theft statute are worth knowing. Under §31.03(c)(1), evidence of recent similar transactions by the actor is admissible to show knowledge or intent, once a not guilty plea raises those issues. Under §31.03(c)(2), accomplice testimony must be corroborated by proof tending to connect the actor to the crime. But knowledge or intent may rest on uncorroborated accomplice testimony.

Why intent and authority are the real fight

The movement of money is usually not in dispute. The records show it. The records do not show the two things the statute requires.

Intent. §31.03(a) requires intent to deprive the owner. §31.01(2) defines that in terms of permanence. Money that was recorded, disclosed, or repaid reads differently than money that was hidden. A bookkeeping error is not an intent.

Authority. §31.03(b)(1) makes appropriation unlawful only if it was without the owner's effective consent. Consent includes consent by a person legally authorized to act for the owner (§31.01(3)). If someone with that authority approved the spending, the State has a problem with an element. The same question drives a §32.45 case, where the issue is the agreement.

If an employer, auditor, or detective starts asking questions

  • An internal interview is not private. What you tell HR, an auditor, or a bank investigator can be written down and handed to police.
  • You do not have to give a statement to law enforcement. Read about your right to remain silent first.
  • Do not delete or change anything. Missing emails, files, or spreadsheets create a new problem on top of the old one.
  • Save the proof of your authority. Approvals, policies, contracts, pay records, and emails showing what you were allowed to do.
  • Do not sign a confession or a repayment agreement before a lawyer reads it. Both get used as evidence.

Where these cases get attacked

Every case is different, and no lawyer can predict a result. The review usually runs along five lines.

  • Authority to use the funds. Who approved what, and could that person act for the owner (§31.01(3))? In a §32.45 case, what did the agreement actually say (§32.45(a)(2))?
  • The value calculation. Is the number fair market value at the time and place (§31.08(a))? Does §31.08(d) allow a deduction for consideration given or a legal interest held?
  • Alternative explanations. Loans, advances, commissions, accepted expense practices, and repaid money all bear on intent to deprive (§31.01(2)).
  • The accuracy of the accounting. A company summary is not raw data. The underlying entries can be tested, and a defense may retain a forensic accountant.
  • A civil dispute treated as a crime. A partnership or contract fight can look like theft in a summary chart. The statute still requires appropriation without effective consent and intent to deprive (§31.03(a), (b)(1)).

Common questions

What do prosecutors have to prove for embezzlement in Texas?

Texas charges it as theft. Under Penal Code section 31.03(a) and (b)(1), the State must prove appropriation of property, without the owner's effective consent, and with intent to deprive. Value sets the grade.

Does the State have to prove intent?

Yes. Section 31.03(a) requires intent to deprive the owner. Section 31.01(2) defines deprive three ways: withholding property permanently or for a long period, restoring it only for compensation, or disposing of it so recovery is unlikely.

What evidence is used in an embezzlement case?

Usually records rather than eyewitnesses. Accounting and bank records, expense files, system access logs, and emails or approvals. Co-workers, supervisors, or auditors are then called to explain them.

Can small amounts be added together into one charge?

Section 31.09 allows it. Amounts obtained pursuant to one scheme or continuing course of conduct may be aggregated. They can come from the same source or several. The conduct is then treated as one offense.

How is the dollar amount decided?

Section 31.08(a) uses fair market value at the time and place of the offense. Replacement cost applies if fair market value cannot be ascertained. Under section 31.08(d), a defendant who proves he gave consideration or held a legal interest gets that amount deducted.
