Intoxication Manslaughter in Texas: What the Turtle Creek Tesla Case Shows About the Charge

One crash at Cedar Springs and Turtle Creek produced two felony charges. The headlines covered the speed and the wreckage. Almost none of them explained the part of the statute that decides these cases: intoxication manslaughter does not require the State to prove the driver meant to hurt anyone at all.

Straight Answer

What is intoxication manslaughter in Texas?

Under Tex. Penal Code §49.08, a person commits intoxication manslaughter by operating a motor vehicle in a public place while intoxicated and, by reason of that intoxication, causing another person's death by accident or mistake. It is a second degree felony: 2 to 20 years in prison and a fine of up to $10,000. The State does not have to prove the driver intended to kill anyone, or even that the driving was reckless. Intoxication plus causation is the whole case.

TL;DR
  • Intoxication manslaughter (§49.08) is a second degree felony: 2 to 20 years, up to a $10,000 fine.
  • Intoxication assault (§49.07), for serious bodily injury rather than death, is a third degree felony: 2 to 10 years.
  • No intent element. Unlike manslaughter under §19.04, the State proves no mental state about the death. That is why prosecutors charge it.
  • "Intoxicated" has two independent definitions under §49.01(2). A blood result under 0.08 does not end the case.
  • A vehicle can be found a deadly weapon, and multiple victims can mean stacked sentences under §3.03(b). Both change the real number far more than the headline range does.
  • The blood warrant affidavit and the vehicle's own recorded data are usually where the case is actually won or lost.

What has been reported

The facts below come from news coverage of the case, principally FOX 4 and WFAA, both reporting from an arrest warrant affidavit. An affidavit is one side's sworn summary written to establish probable cause. It is not evidence at trial, and it is not a finding by anyone.

On the evening of Sunday, November 30, 2025, a 2025 Tesla travelling south on Cedar Springs Road near Turtle Creek Boulevard in Uptown Dallas left the roadway. According to police, the car struck two trees and a median, went airborne, and hit a Porsche stopped at a red light. Emma Hackney, 29, died. She was an SMU Law graduate and an attorney at Jackson Walker LLP. A partner at the firm, Wade Cooper, described her publicly as a colleague with a bright future and said the firm was heartbroken. A male passenger in her vehicle survived with injuries. The driver's mother, a passenger in the Tesla, was also injured.

The driver, Preston Petty, then 26, was arrested and charged with intoxication manslaughter and intoxication assault causing serious bodily injury. Reporting indicates an officer noted an odor of alcohol, that Petty did not pass field sobriety testing, and that a blood warrant was obtained after he declined to give a sample. Petty told officers he lost control because his mother reached for the phone in his hand. He denied being intoxicated, while stating he was driving home to drink wine. A witness described the Tesla as travelling far too fast.

The charge, element by element

Prosecutors reach for §49.08 in fatal crash cases for a reason that has nothing to do with how bad the driving looked. Read the elements:

  1. The person operated a motor vehicle
  2. in a public place
  3. while intoxicated, and
  4. by reason of that intoxication caused the death of another

That is the entire offense. There is no fifth element about state of mind. The statute expressly contemplates that the death happens "by accident or mistake." A driver who was genuinely trying to be careful, who never saw the other car, who is devastated by what happened, has no defense in those facts alone. Remorse is a punishment issue, not a guilt issue.

Why this is not the same as manslaughter

People often assume a fatal crash gets charged as manslaughter. Under §19.04, manslaughter requires the State to prove the defendant recklessly caused the death: that he was aware of a substantial and unjustifiable risk and consciously disregarded it. That is a real mental state, and it can be genuinely difficult to prove in a single-moment driving case.

Intoxication manslaughter removes that problem for the prosecution entirely. Both offenses are second degree felonies carrying the same 2 to 20 year range, but one requires proof of a culpable mental state and the other does not.

The practical consequence: in an intoxication manslaughter case, almost every defensive dollar goes to the intoxication element and the causal link, because those are the only two doors in the room.

"Intoxicated" means two different things

This is where cases turn, and where public understanding is furthest off. §49.01(2) gives the State two independent routes:

  • The impairment definition. Not having the normal use of mental or physical faculties by reason of alcohol, a controlled substance, a drug, a dangerous drug, a combination of them, or any other substance.
  • The per se definition. An alcohol concentration of 0.08 or more.

They are alternatives, not a two-part test. A blood result below 0.08 does not end a case, because prosecutors can still argue loss of normal faculties using the driving, the video, the officer's observations, and any drugs in the system. Equally, a driver who seems composed on video can still be convicted on a number alone. Any commentary that treats the case as a simple question of whether the blood came back over the limit is describing a test that does not exist.

The second charge, and why it is not a technicality

The intoxication assault count under §49.07 covers a different victim: someone who suffered serious bodily injury rather than death. It is a third degree felony, 2 to 10 years and a fine up to $10,000. "Serious bodily injury" is defined in §1.07(a)(46) and means injury creating a substantial risk of death, or causing serious permanent disfigurement or protracted loss or impairment of a bodily member or organ. It is a meaningfully higher bar than "hurt," and it is contestable with medical records.

The reason a second count matters so much is §3.03(b). For most offenses arising out of the same criminal episode, Texas sentences run concurrently. Intoxication manslaughter and intoxication assault are specifically carved out: a judge may order them stacked, one after the other. In a case with a death and a separately injured victim, the practical exposure is not the 20-year headline. It can be 20 plus 10.

What the punishment range does not tell you

"Two to twenty" sounds like a wide field with a soft floor. Three separate mechanisms narrow it hard in a case like this one.

The deadly weapon finding. Under §1.07(a)(17) a deadly weapon includes anything that, in the manner of its use, is capable of causing death or serious bodily injury. Texas courts routinely uphold that finding for a motor vehicle. An affirmative finding changes parole eligibility: the person must serve half the sentence before becoming eligible, and good conduct time does not count toward it. Two defendants with identical 12-year sentences can serve very different amounts of time based on that one finding, which is why it is litigated as its own battle rather than treated as a footnote.

Restricted community supervision. Intoxication manslaughter appears on the list in Tex. Code Crim. Proc. art. 42A.054. A judge cannot place someone on regular community supervision after a conviction for it. A jury can recommend it, which is one of the real reasons these cases go to a jury for punishment, and where it is granted the law still requires a minimum period of confinement as a condition.

Stacking. Covered above, and the single largest driver of the difference between the headline number and the real one.

The blood warrant is usually the fight

Reporting indicates a warrant was obtained after a refusal. That sequence is standard in Dallas County and it is also where a very large share of these cases are won or lost.

Texas cannot simply take blood because someone died. The Texas Court of Criminal Appeals held in State v. Villarreal (2014) that the mandatory blood draw provision does not by itself create an exception to the Fourth Amendment warrant requirement. The U.S. Supreme Court held in Missouri v. McNeely (2013) that alcohol naturally leaving the bloodstream is not automatically the kind of emergency that excuses a warrant, and in Birchfield v. North Dakota (2016) that a blood draw cannot be justified as a search incident to arrest. So officers get a warrant.

Getting a warrant and getting a good warrant are different things. The affidavit has to contain specific facts establishing probable cause to believe this driver was intoxicated. Affidavits written at speed, in the middle of a chaotic fatal scene, frequently lean on recycled boilerplate: the standard triad of bloodshot eyes, slurred speech, odor of alcohol, with little that is specific to the person. When the affidavit is thin, the blood result and everything derived from it are on the table in a motion to suppress. Our guide on how motions to suppress work in Texas covers that process, and what refusing a test actually triggers covers the license side.

The chain after the draw matters too: who drew it, under what conditions, how it was stored, how it was transported, how the lab handled it, whether the analyst can be cross-examined. None of that is exotic. It is the ordinary work of defending a blood case.

The car is a witness now

The detail in the reporting about data from the vehicle is the genuinely modern part of this case, and it is worth understanding because it now appears in nearly every serious crash file.

Two different sources are usually in play. The first is the event data recorder, the "black box" that most passenger vehicles carry and that federal regulation standardises. It captures a short window around a crash: speed, throttle, braking, steering input, seatbelt status, sometimes change in velocity at impact. The second, specific to vehicles like Tesla, is dashcam and sentry footage written to a USB drive, plus telemetry the manufacturer may hold.

For the defense this data cuts both ways, and that is the point. It can devastate a case by showing speed no witness could have estimated accurately. It can also be the strongest evidence a defendant has. Precise steering and braking inputs in the seconds before impact speak directly to whether someone had the normal use of their physical faculties. If a driver's account is that a passenger grabbed at the phone, the recorded steering trace either supports that or it does not, in a way no testimony can match.

Two practical points follow. Data has to be preserved immediately, before a vehicle is released, repaired, or the recorder is overwritten. And how it was extracted matters: whether a warrant covered it, who performed the download, and whether the tooling produced a verifiable copy are all live legal questions, not just technical ones.

Key takeaways

Key Takeaways
  • Intoxication manslaughter is a second degree felony with no intent element. Intoxication plus causation is the case.
  • "Intoxicated" has two independent definitions. A number below 0.08 does not end the matter, and a calm video does not either.
  • A second count for a second victim can be stacked, so real exposure often exceeds the headline range.
  • A deadly weapon finding changes when parole eligibility begins and is worth fighting on its own.
  • The blood warrant affidavit and the vehicle's recorded data are where these cases are usually decided.
  • Evidence in crash cases degrades fast. The first week matters more than any other.

Frequently asked questions

What is intoxication manslaughter in Texas?

Under Tex. Penal Code §49.08, it is operating a motor vehicle in a public place while intoxicated and, by reason of that intoxication, causing another person's death by accident or mistake. It is a second degree felony punishable by 2 to 20 years and a fine of up to $10,000.

Does the State have to prove the driver intended to kill someone?

No. This is the most misunderstood part of the charge. There is no intent element and no recklessness element as to the death. The statute itself says the death may occur by accident or mistake.

What is the difference between intoxication manslaughter and manslaughter?

Manslaughter under §19.04 requires proof that the defendant recklessly caused the death, meaning conscious disregard of a substantial and unjustifiable risk. Intoxication manslaughter requires no mental state about the death at all. Both are second degree felonies, but one is materially easier for the State to prove.

What is intoxication assault in Texas?

Under §49.07, causing serious bodily injury to another by accident or mistake while operating a motor vehicle in a public place while intoxicated. It is a third degree felony: 2 to 10 years and a fine of up to $10,000.

Can police take your blood after a fatal crash in Texas?

Generally only with a warrant, valid consent, or a genuine emergency. State v. Villarreal held the mandatory draw statute is not itself a warrant exception, and Missouri v. McNeely held that alcohol dissipating in the blood is not automatically an emergency. Officers therefore apply for a warrant, and the affidavit supporting it is often the first thing challenged.

Is probation possible for intoxication manslaughter?

It is limited. The offense is listed in art. 42A.054, so a judge cannot grant regular community supervision after conviction. A jury can recommend it, and where granted the law requires a minimum period of confinement as a condition.

Can a car be a deadly weapon in Texas?

Yes. Under §1.07(a)(17) anything capable of causing death or serious bodily injury in the manner of its use qualifies, and Texas courts routinely uphold the finding for motor vehicles. It matters because it delays parole eligibility.

Can data from my car be used against me?

Yes. Event data recorders capture speed, braking, throttle and steering around a crash, and vehicles like Tesla also store camera footage. That same data sometimes helps the defense, which is why preserving it early matters regardless of which way it points.
